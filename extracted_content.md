# Program Overview

RAC has invested in Claude Teams to give all staff access to an AI assistant that can help with day-to-day work. This training program is designed to take you from your first conversation with Claude through to using it confidently in your role.
The program is split into three sessions, each building on the last. You don’t need any technical background – just an open mind and a willingness to experiment.

## What is Claude?

Claude is an AI assistant made by Anthropic. Think of it as a very knowledgeable colleague you can chat with – it can help draft emails, summarise documents, answer questions, brainstorm ideas, analyse data, and much more. It won’t replace your expertise, but it can save you significant time on tasks that would otherwise take hours.
With Claude Teams, your conversations are private and are not used to train the AI. This is different from free consumer AI tools. RAC’s data stays within RAC’s account.

## What You’ll Need

  - Your RAC email address (used to log in)
  - A computer, phone, or tablet with internet access
  - Access to claude.ai or the Claude desktop/mobile app
  - An open mind – no coding or technical skills required

## 1.1  Logging In & Finding Your Way Around

The best way to learn is by doing. We’ll start with some simple, practical examples that everyone can relate to:

  - Explain something: “Explain what a Purchase Order is in simple terms for someone new to finance.”

| Hands-On Exercise Each participant will try 3–4 prompts of their own choosing. Pick something real from your work – a tricky email you need to write, a question you’ve been meaning to research, or an idea you want to think through. |

## 1.3  Writing Better Prompts

The quality of what Claude gives you depends on what you ask. Here are the basics of getting good results:
Be Specific
Weak: “Write an email.”
Better: “Write a polite email to a supplier letting them know their invoice for quarry materials has been approved and payment will be processed within 14 days.”
Give Context
Claude doesn’t know what you’re working on unless you tell it. Share relevant background – who the audience is, what the purpose is, what tone you need.
Ask for Changes
Your first result won’t always be perfect. That’s normal. Say things like “make it shorter”, “use a friendlier tone”, or “add a section about timelines”. Think of it as a conversation, not a one-shot request.
Set the Format
Tell Claude how you want the answer: “Give me this as bullet points”, “Keep it under 200 words”, “Format this as a table”.

| RAC Example Instead of: “Tell me about grants” — Try: “I work at Rirratjingu Aboriginal Corporation in Nhulunbuy, NT. We’re looking for federal grants related to Indigenous language preservation. Can you outline what’s typically required in an application to the NIAA?” |
| --- |


## 1.4  Setting Up Your Preferences

Claude has a personal preferences feature that helps it understand who you are and how you like to work. We’ll walk everyone through pasting RAC’s background text into their profile:
  - Go to Settings (bottom-left of the sidebar)
  - Click General, then find the Profile section
  - Paste the RAC preferences text (provided separately) into the personal preferences box
  - Click Save
Once set up, Claude will understand RAC’s context – our entities, location, cultural context – without you having to explain it every time. You can also add your own role-specific details like “I work in finance” or “I manage the career pathways program”.

## 1.5  Privacy & Important Notes

  - Each person’s conversations are private to them
  - Use your Teams account for work only – personal Claude accounts have different privacy settings
  - Always review Claude’s output before using it – it’s a tool, not a decision-maker
  - Don’t share passwords, banking details, or highly sensitive personal information

  - A “Board Papers” project with formatting requirements and example papers

## 3.3  Advanced Prompting Techniques

Building on Session 1’s basics, here are techniques for getting even better results:
Give Claude a Role
“You are an experienced grant writer who specialises in Indigenous community development funding. Help me draft the project description section for a language preservation grant.”
Provide Examples
Show Claude what good looks like: “Here’s an example of the format I need: [paste example]. Now create a similar one for [your topic].”
Break Down Complex Tasks
Instead of asking for everything at once, work through it step by step: “First, outline the structure. Then let’s work on each section one at a time.”
Ask Claude to Think It Through
“Before you answer, think through the pros and cons of each option and explain your reasoning.” This often produces more thoughtful, nuanced responses.
Iterate and Refine
Treat it as a back-and-forth collaboration. Your first prompt gets you started; follow-up prompts refine the result. The conversation is the process.

# Session 4A: Claude CoWork

Putting Claude to work on your computer — end-to-end task execution without coding.

| Duration | 90–120 minutes (including hands-on practice) |
| --- | --- |
| Audience | Non-technical staff who completed Sessions 1–3 — recommended cohort of 10–15 |
| Objectives | Understand what CoWork is and how it differs from Claude chat; set up a working folder; execute multi-step tasks with local files; run parallel tasks; understand when to use CoWork vs Claude chat |


## 4A.1  What is CoWork — and Why It Changes Things

Up to now, you have been using Claude as a chat interface — you ask, Claude answers, you copy the result. CoWork is fundamentally different. It is Claude acting as a digital teammate that can access files directly on your computer, plan and execute multi-step tasks on your behalf, spin up multiple sub-tasks running in parallel, and save its outputs directly to your local folders — no copying and pasting required.
Think of the difference this way:

| Claude Chat (what you know) | Claude CoWork (what's new) |
| --- | --- |
| You upload files manually each time | Claude reads files directly from your computer |
| One question, one answer | Claude plans and executes multiple steps automatically |
| You copy output from the chat | Finished files are saved directly to your folders |
| Works in  Anthropic's  cloud | Works with your local file system |
| Great for thinking and drafting | Great for getting work done end-to-end |


## 4A.2  Setting Up CoWork

CoWork is available inside Claude Desktop. Before your first session, you will need to complete a short setup:
Download and install Claude Desktop from claude.ai if you have not already done so
Open Claude Desktop — you will see three tabs at the top: Chat, CoWork, and Claude Code
Click CoWork to switch to the CoWork interface
Select a working folder — this is the folder on your computer where Claude will read from and save to. Create a dedicated folder for this (e.g. RAC CoWork Projects)
That is it — CoWork is now ready to use

| Important — Working Folder Claude CoWork can only access files inside the working folder you select. Before starting any task, make sure all the files Claude will need are already saved in that folder. Think of it as your project desk — put everything on the desk before asking Claude to start work. |
| --- |


## 4A.3  Your First CoWork Task

The best way to understand CoWork is to see it handle something that would take you 30–60 minutes manually. We will start with a practical RAC scenario:

| Hands-On Exercise 1 — Document Summarisation Pipeline Save 3–4 PDF or Word documents into your working folder (e.g. a policy document, a report, and a set of meeting notes). Then prompt CoWork: "Read all the documents in this folder and create a single summary document that captures the key points from each one. Save it as RAC-Summary.docx." Watch how CoWork plans the task, reads each file, and saves the finished document — without you doing anything after the initial prompt. |
| --- |


## 4A.4  Using Your Existing Claude Projects with CoWork

If you have already built Claude Projects with custom instructions and reference files, you can bring these directly into CoWork — giving you the best of both worlds: the persistent context and instructions from your Project, plus CoWork's local file access and autonomous execution.
In Claude Desktop, open your existing Claude Project in the Chat tab
Click the option to add this Project to CoWork — this copies your Project instructions and files into the CoWork session
Select your working folder
CoWork will now follow your Project instructions automatically for every task

| RAC Example — Board Paper Preparation Set up a Claude Project with RAC's board paper format and style requirements. Add it to CoWork. Drop the raw inputs (executive summaries, financial figures, program updates) into your working folder. Prompt: "Using the board paper format from the project instructions, compile these documents into a draft board paper and save it to this folder." CoWork handles the rest. |
| --- |


## 4A.5  Parallel Tasks — Getting More Done at Once

One of CoWork's most powerful features is the ability to run multiple tasks at the same time. Rather than doing things sequentially, CoWork can spin up sub-agents to work in parallel — like having several assistants each handling a different piece of a project simultaneously.
You do not need to configure this technically. Simply describe the parallel tasks in your prompt and CoWork will organise the work automatically:

| RAC Example — Quarterly Review Package "I need to prepare the quarterly review package. Please do three things at once: (1) summarise the Q1 program reports from the documents in this folder, (2) create a financial summary table from the Xero export spreadsheet, and (3) draft an executive summary combining both. Save all three as separate files." |
| --- |

CoWork will plan the tasks, assign sub-agents, work through them in parallel, and save all three files — typically in a fraction of the time it would take sequentially.

## 4A.7  What CoWork Cannot Do — and When to Use Chat Instead

CoWork is powerful but it is not always the right tool. Use CoWork when you have files to process, multi-step tasks to execute, or outputs that need to be saved locally. Stick with Claude Chat when you are brainstorming, thinking through problems, having a back-and-forth conversation, or just need a quick answer. The two tools complement each other — CoWork does the work, Chat does the thinking.

| Tip — Start Simple Your first CoWork tasks should be straightforward. Once you are confident with how it works, gradually increase the complexity. A common mistake is giving CoWork a very  complex task before understanding how it handles simpler ones. Build up your confidence with the tool first. |

# Session 4B: Claude Skills & Agents

Building reusable AI workflows that the whole team can use — skill libraries, plugins, and agentic automation.

| Duration | 120 minutes (demonstration-heavy with hands-on build exercise) |
| --- | --- |
| Audience | Staff already working in Claude Code — recommended cohort of 2–5, primarily Duane and technically-inclined staff |
| Objectives | Understand what Claude Skills are and why they matter; build a first RAC skill from an existing SOP; understand plugins and how to package skills for the team; know when to use sub- agents  vs CoWork |


## 4B.1  The Difference Between Using Claude and Building with Claude

Everything in Sessions 1–3 and 4A is about using Claude — asking it to do things for you. Session 4B is about building with Claude — creating reusable systems that execute your workflows automatically, consistently, and at scale. The distinction matters because:
A great prompt you write once has to be written again next time
A Claude Skill encodes that prompt, workflow, and context permanently — you never have to write it again
Skills can be shared with the whole team so everyone benefits from the work you put in once
Skills can be combined to handle complex multi-step processes automatically
Think of it like the difference between cooking a meal versus writing a recipe. Session 4A was cooking. Session 4B is writing recipes that anyone — including Claude — can follow reliably every time.

## 4B.2  What is a Claude Skill?

A Claude Skill is a structured file (created inside Claude Code) that defines a specific workflow — its purpose, the steps it follows, the tools it uses, the format of its output, and any reference materials or examples it draws on. Once created, a skill can be invoked by name whenever that workflow is needed, either manually or automatically by Claude when it recognises the task.
Skills sit alongside your normal Claude Code setup in a designated skills folder. They are written once and used many times. Some examples of what a RAC skill library could contain:

| Skill Name | What It Does | Who Uses It |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grant Application Drafter | Takes a grant URL, extracts requirements, matches against RAC's profile, drafts an application section | Community Programs, Leadership |
| Board Paper Formatter | Takes raw inputs and formats them into RAC's board paper template with correct structure and headings | Administration, Leadership |
| Financial Anomaly Report | Reads a Xero export, identifies anomalies vs prior period, produces a one-page summary | Finance |
| Program Acquittal Writer | Converts outcome data and expenditure figures into a formatted acquittal narrative | Community Programs |
| Correspondence Processor | Reads incoming correspondence, classifies by type and urgency, drafts response templates | Administration |
| Quarterly Review Package | Combines program reports, financial data, and executive summary into a review package | Leadership, all teams |


## 4B.3  Building Your First RAC Skill

The best skills are built from processes that already exist — your SOPs, your proven templates, your best examples of past work. Claude reads these and encodes the workflow into the skill. The richer the inputs, the better the skill.
Method 1 — From an Existing SOP
If RAC already has a documented process for something, you can turn it directly into a skill:
Open Claude Code and navigate to your RAC skills folder (or create one)
Prompt Claude: "Create a new skill called [Skill Name] that follows the process in this SOP document. The skill should [describe the output]. Use [any tools or MCPs needed]." — paste or attach your SOP
Claude reads the SOP, understands the workflow, and writes the skill file
Review the skill file in your IDE — check that the steps match your process
Test by invoking the skill: type the slash command or ask Claude to use it
Method 2 — From Your Best Examples
If you do not have a formal SOP but you have examples of great past work, you can give those to Claude instead:
Gather 3–5 examples of the best output of this type RAC has produced (e.g. your best grant applications, your clearest board papers)
Prompt Claude: "Analyse these examples, identify the patterns and structure that make them effective, and create a reusable skill that follows those patterns."
Claude extracts the implicit process from your examples and encodes it as a skill

| Hands-On Build Exercise We will build one RAC skill together during the session. Bring an existing SOP or 2–3 examples of a common work output (a report, a grant section, a correspondence template). By the end of the  session  you will have a working skill that any team member can invoke. |
| --- |


## 4B.4  Combining Skills — Multi-Skill Workflows

The real power of a skill library emerges when skills are used together. Rather than doing one task, you can give Claude a complex goal and it will automatically identify which skills to use and in what order — or run them in parallel for efficiency.
For example, a quarterly review workflow might use three skills together:
Skill 1: Program Report Summariser — processes all team reports
Skill 2: Financial Anomaly Report — processes the Xero data
Skill 3: Quarterly Review Package — combines both into the final document
Claude orchestrates these automatically. You give it the goal; it plans the work, assigns each skill, runs them (in parallel where possible), and delivers the finished package. What might take a full day of manual work becomes a 15-minute automated pipeline.

## 4B.5  Packaging Skills as a Plugin

Once you have built a skill library for RAC, you can package all your skills into a Plugin — a single bundle that can be shared with any team member and imported into their CoWork or Claude Code environment in one step, rather than installing skills one by one.
In Claude Code, prompt Claude to package your RAC skills into a plugin: "Create a plugin called RAC Workflow Tools that includes all the skills in this folder. Add a slash command for [common task] that triggers [specific skills]."
Claude reads your skills and packages them into the plugin format
Share the plugin zip file with team members
Team members import it in CoWork or Claude Code via Add Plugin — one click and all skills are available
This is how the work you do in Session 4B multiplies across the team. You build once; everyone benefits. Over time, RAC's plugin becomes an ever-growing library of institutional knowledge encoded into reliable, repeatable workflows.

## 4B.6  Sub-Agents — When One Claude Is Not Enough

For very complex tasks, Claude can act as a team lead and spin up sub-agents — separate Claude instances each working on a specific part of the problem simultaneously. Sub-agents are triggered automatically when you describe a task that has clearly parallel components:

| RAC Example — Annual Report Preparation "Prepare the annual report package. Task 1: Summarise all program outcome reports from this folder. Task 2: Process the financial statements and create the financial highlights section. Task 3: Draft the CEO's message based on the key themes from Tasks 1 and 2. Run Tasks 1 and 2 in parallel, then complete Task 3 once both are done." Claude will spin up sub-agents for Tasks 1 and 2, wait for both to complete, then synthesise into Task 3. |
| --- |


| Sub-Agents vs CoWork Sub-agents are most useful for complex tasks with clearly parallel components where the outputs need to be combined at the end. For most day-to-day work,  CoWork's  built- in parallelism (Session 4A) is simpler to use and sufficient. Sub-agents in Claude Code give you more control and are better suited to building automated pipelines that run repeatedly. |
| --- |


## 4B.7  The RAC Skill Library — A Long-Term Asset

Every skill you build is a permanent asset for RAC. Unlike a well-crafted prompt that lives in one person's head, a skill is documented, shareable, and improvable over time. As RAC's Claude usage matures, the skill library becomes a repository of institutional knowledge — encoding how RAC does things, in RAC's voice and format, drawing on RAC's context.
Some principles for building a skill library that lasts:
Start with your highest-frequency, highest-effort tasks — the ones that eat the most time
Always base skills on real RAC processes — SOPs, templates, or proven examples
Include RAC's context files in every skill so outputs are always on-brand and culturally appropriate
Review and refine skills based on team feedback — treat them as living documents
Version control your skills folder so changes are tracked over time
